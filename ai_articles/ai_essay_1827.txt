suggests the face is simply a product of natural geological formations and processes occurring on mars over billions of years. some of the key claims supporting the idea that the face is natural include its vague resemblance once seen up close. early lowresolution photos from orbit made it look like an anthropic construct, but higher resolution images revealed it to be an indistinct hillside that only superficially resembles a human face from certain angles. the article also notes there are similar mesas and hillsides on mars that look vaguely facelike without any real features. this implies facelike landforms can be produced naturally through erosion over eons without any intelligence involved. additional evidence cited in the article is how wind erosion shapes the land on mars. strong winds pick up and move particles of dust and ice across the surface, slowly wearing away at rock and soil. over massive stretches of geological time, this could reasonably create shallow clefts and cavities that give the impression of eyes, nose and mouth when viewed remotely. the article notes how scientists have modeled this process and their simulations match what is seen on the actual face. this lends credibility to the idea that natural wind erosion alone could generate the loose likeness to a human face. in conclusion, while early lowquality images sparked speculation of an artificial construct, upon closer examination the evidence suggests the face on mars is most plausibly a random, vague arrangement of natural landforms shaped simply by environmental processes over martian history. the resemblance is superficial and fleeting, an example of pareidolia rather than proof of an alien artifact or structure. for these reasons, the weight of scientific evidence supports classifying the face on mars as a naturallyoccurring geological formation rather than something created by an ancient alien civilization.
