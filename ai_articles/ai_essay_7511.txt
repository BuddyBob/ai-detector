the quality of instruction at colleges and universities is a top priority for students, educators, and employers. there are various ways to improve the quality of instruction, and one of them is to require all faculty to spend time working outside the academic world in professions relevant to the courses they teach. while this proposal may seem appealing, it is not without its challenges and limitations. in this essay, i will argue that although there are some benefits to requiring faculty to have work experience outside academia, it is not a practical solution for improving the quality of instruction. on the one hand, having faculty members with practical experience in their fields could bring several benefits to the classroom. for example, faculty members would be able to offer realworld examples that students could relate to and learn from. they could also share their insights and perspectives on industry trends, challenges, and opportunities. this would enable students to gain a more nuanced and holistic understanding of their subject matter, beyond what they can learn from textbooks or lectures alone. moreover, faculty members with industry experience could help students prepare for their future careers by providing them with more relevant and uptodate skills, knowledge, and networks. on the other hand, requiring all faculty members to have work experience outside academia raises several practical and ethical concerns. firstly, it would be difficult to implement and enforce such a requirement, given the wide variety of courses and disciplines that exist in higher education. not all courses have direct counterparts in the industry, and not all faculty members would be willing or able to work outside academia. secondly, it would be unfair to penalize faculty members who have devoted their entire careers to teaching and research, but may not have had the opportunity to work in the industry. this could also lead to a brain drain of experienced and dedicated educators, who may feel undervalued and demotivated. thirdly, it could create conflicts of interest and biases if faculty members have personal or financial interests in the industries they work in. this could undermine the objectivity and credibility of higher education and erode public trust. furthermore, there are alternative ways to improve the quality of instruction that do not require all faculty members to have work experience outside academia. for example, colleges and universities could offer faculty members more opportunities for professional development, such as attending conferences, workshops, and training sessions. they could also provide them with better resources and support, such as access to cuttingedge technologies, research funding, and teaching assistants. additionally, they could foster a culture of collaboration and innovation among faculty members, by encouraging them to share their best practices, ideas, and feedback with one another. these measures could help faculty members stay uptodate with the latest trends and practices, and inspire them to continuously improve their teaching and research. in conclusion, while the idea of requiring all faculty members to have work experience outside academia may seem appealing, it is not a practical or ethical solution for improving the quality of instruction. instead, colleges and universities should explore alternative ways to support and empower their faculty members to become better educators and researchers. by doing so, they can create a more inclusive, innovative, and impactful higher education system that benefits students, faculty, and society as a whole.
