keeping the electoral college is that it encourages candidates to build broader coalitions and campaign in more states. however, research shows that presidential campaigns increasingly focus their energies only on swing states that could vote either way. this means over  of campaign visits and general election advertising are focused on just a handful of states. using the popular vote might encourage candidates to appeal to a broader range of viewpoints across the entire nation rather than select regions. it is also argued that the electoral college system maintains the federal character of our system and proportional state power. however, deciding the presidency solely based on the national popular vote could be said to better reflect the principles of direct democracy and majority rule. the president is meant to represent and serve all americans, not just those in swing states. recent close national elections have underscored how someone could become president despite losing the nationwide popular vote. this does not seem consistent with the standard of one person, one vote that americans expect. while smaller states rightfully fear losing influence, moving to a popular vote may not diminish their voices as much as predicted. candidates would still have to build diverse coalitions and work to turn out voters in urban and rural areas alike. a national campaign focused on issues important across the whole country could help address some of the divisions that plague our society. holding elections according to who actually receives the most votes could strengthen perceptions of legitimacy and fairness regarding such a consequential office. in conclusion, while reasonable arguments can be made on both sides of this issue, i believe changing to a nationwide popular vote system for electing the president would better uphold democratic principles and encourage policies representative of americans as a whole. the electoral college risks being inconsistent
