the use of facial recognition technology like the facial action coding system to read students emotional expressions in the classroom is problematic and an invasion of privacy. while identifying students emotions could provide teachers insights to better engage students, this type of monitoring also raises serious ethical concerns. on one hand, the technology aims to help teachers understand how their lessons are landing with students on an emotional level. knowing when students appear bored, confused or frustrated could help teachers adapt their instruction in realtime to improve engagement and learning outcomes. proponents might argue this leads to a more personalized education. however, constantly monitoring students facial expressions and private emotions without their consent crosses an important line. in the classroom, students should feel free to naturally experience a full range of reactions without fearing a computer system is overanalyzing every microexpression. emotions are complex and personal, and automatic classification by an algorithm may misconstrue true feelings or lack important context about individuals. additionally, using this type of surveillance could undermine the studentteacher relationship built on trust. students may feel less comfortable openly expressing themselves if they know a computer is reporting details about their emotional state back to teachers and administrators. over time, this lack of privacy could negatively impact psychological development and wellbeing. in balancing the potential benefits with serious risks to student privacy and development, the downsides of conducting ongoing computerized emotional surveillance in the classroom seem to outweigh the advantages. while technology that provides insights to improve teaching has merit, reading students private emotions without consent goes too far. schools should pursue alternatives that respect student privacy and allow natural human relationships in the learning process to thrive. overall, the concerns about students wellbeing and rights outweigh using this technology without meaningful consent in the classroom setting.
